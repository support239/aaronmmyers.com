Ethan: He won’t go and get a job. He sits around the house on games and stays in his room most of the day. It's like he’s completely entitled.

Aaron: How old is he?

Ethan: He’s 17. We just moved to a new state, so he doesn’t really have many friends here. But he won't even try.

Sarah: (Looking at the ground, obviously worried about the future of her son.) I’m just terrified of pushing him away. If we set hard boundaries, I feel like we’ll lose him completely.

Ethan: (Crossing his arms tightly) He’s taken a liking to driving for Uber to make money. But he’s barely making anything doing it, and it's certainly not a long-term plan.

Aaron: I see. And how does that make you feel?

Ethan: It pisses me off. I owned my own business. I was away working most of the time when he was young, slaving away and helping the family financially. Now I feel this massive resentment toward him for his ingratitude and lack of contribution.

Aaron: Can I tell you something that might sting a little bit?

Ethan: Go ahead.

Aaron: You’re taking his behavior personally. Out of a survey of 4,000 people, the top two reasons people take things personally are that they want the other person to see their perspective, or they feel they have something to lose. What’s your biggest fear here?

Ethan: (Pauses, staring down at the floor for a long moment, his tone softening) My biggest fear in reaching my goal of helping him leave the nest and get a job... is that my own skill and ability as a father are lacking. I know I take it personally.

Aaron: Thank you for admitting that. Here is the 3-step cycle you need to follow:

1 Seek to understand where he’s coming from.

2 Notice his response—whether he gets defensive, frustrated, annoyed, silent, or fights back.

3 Understand that it is only his perspective and stop taking it personally.

Ethan: The third step is the hardest part! He needs consequences!

Aaron: Yes, but consequences can’t be done out of leverage and control over his living situation. His love language is gifts, so taking things away feels like a harsh, punishing blow to a teenage boy. You have to judge his receptivity first. When he’s defensive, bite your tongue and wait. When he is receptive, explain natural, objective consequences based on how his current actions aren't reaching his long-term values.

(The next time we met for a coaching session.)

Aaron: How did it go this week?

Ethan: I spent time with him. I judged his receptivity, bit my tongue when he was defensive, and realized he’s actually trying in his own way.

Aaron: And what happened when you stopped forcing it?

Ethan: The feedback was a way more open dialogue. Once he realized I wasn't attacking him, he opened up about his struggles and doubts with growing up. He admitted he’s struggling to find the will and gets severely discouraged when he doesn't get a job offer.

Sarah: (Tearing up) He was just afraid. He felt like he couldn't measure up to us.

Ethan: He told me straight out that he fears disappointing me because he isn't as successful as me. I realized his skills and values are elsewhere right now. I wished he were farther ahead, but I realized this is going to take some time.

Aaron: That's a huge breakthrough. What did you do next?

Ethan: Instead of forcing my uber-successful business background on him, I humbled myself. I offered to help him with a different business venture than driving for Uber, to help him build his business acumen little by little. He needed small steps, because he wasn't ready to think like a thriving businessman overnight.

Aaron: Very good! You found a synthesis of principles rather than using leverage.

Sarah: The dialogue in our house is totally open now. The relationship has improved dramatically since Ethan started using the 3-step cycle and judging receptivity before giving advice.

Ethan: When I showed him options with objective, natural consequences instead of taking things away, he actually listened. He even took another stab at building a venture, and he knows I'm on his team now instead of judging him.

Aaron: That’s when parents are truly able to instill wisdom and experience—when everyone involved is receptive and collaborative.

Sarah: We can’t thank you enough, Aaron. We can work things out together now, and we're actually communicating like a family again.

Ethan: I admit I was too harsh. I was too quick to give advice and too slow to listen. But now we have a way better relationship, and we can navigate his future together step by step.

When we stop taking a teenager's defensiveness personally, we can shift from trying to control them to actually guiding them. Influence doesn't come from leverage—it comes from timing, receptivity, and connection.
