(The story below is real, but the names have been changed for confidentiality.)

Ethan: He won’t go and get a job. He sits around the house on games and stays in his room most of the day. He’s completely entitled.

Aaron: How old is he?

Ethan: He’s 17. We just moved to a new state, so he doesn’t really have many friends here. But he won't even try.

Sarah: (Looking down anxiously) I’m just terrified of pushing him away. If we set hard boundaries, I feel like we’ll lose him completely.

Ethan: (Crossing his arms ) He’s taken a liking to driving for Uber to make money. But he’s barely making anything doing it.

Aaron: I see. And how does that make you feel?

Ethan: It pisses me off. I owned my own business. I was away working most of the time when he was young, helping the family financially. Now I feel this massive resentment toward him for his ingratitude and lack of contribution.

Aaron: Can I tell you something that might sting a little bit?

Ethan: Go ahead.

Aaron: You’re taking his behavior personally. Out of a survey of 4,000 people, the top two reasons people take things personally are that they want the other person to see their perspective, or they feel they have something to lose. What’s your biggest fear here?

Ethan: (Pauses , staring down at the floor ) My biggest fear in trying to help him leave the nest and get a job... is that my own skill and ability as a father are lacking.

Aaron: Thank you for admitting that. Here is the 3-step cycle you need:

1 Seek to understand where he’s coming from.

2 Notice his response—whether he gets defensive, frustrated, annoyed, silent, or fights back.

3 Understand it’s only his perspective and stop taking it personally.

Ethan: The third step is the hardest part! He needs consequences!

Aaron: Yes, but consequences can’t be done out of leverage and control over his living situation. His love language is gifts, so taking things away feels like a harsh punishment to a teen. You have to judge his receptivity first. When he’s defensive, bite your tongue. When he is receptive, explain natural, objective consequences.

(The next time we met.)

Aaron: How did it go?

Ethan: I spent time with him. I judged his receptivity, bit my tongue, and realized he’s actually trying.

Aaron: And what happened when you stopped forcing it?

Ethan: The feedback was a way more open dialogue. He opened up about his struggles and doubts with growing up. He admitted he’s struggling to find the will and gets discouraged when he doesn't get a job.

Sarah: (Tearing up ) He was just afraid.

Ethan: He told me he fears disappointing me because he isn't as successful as me. I realized his skills and values are elsewhere. Instead of forcing my uber-successful business background on him, I humbled myself. I offered to help him with a different venture than driving for Uber to build his business acumen little by little.

Aaron: Very good! You found a synthesis of principles rather than using leverage.

Sarah: The dialogue in our house is totally open now. The relationship has improved dramatically.

When we stop taking a teenager's defensiveness personally, we can shift from trying to control them to actually guiding them. Influence doesn't come from leverage—it comes from timing, receptivity, and connection.
